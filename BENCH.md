# BENCH.md: every experiment, kept or not

## How to read this

- **Scorer** is the only number that counts: the track page's time for a commit, on the hidden ~20M-event practice set.
- **x86 A/B** is what decides whether a change is kept: the `bench` workflow builds two commits with the Dockerfile and runs them interleaved on one GitHub x86-64 machine (2 cores / 4 threads, stand-in 20M-event dataset from `src/bin/synth.rs`). Times are the in-process total in ms, minimum of 5 runs, base → head. Noise between repeats is about 1%. The CPU differs from run to run, so only compare within a row.
- The workflow's 4-thread number has two threads to a core; its **one-thread** number has a core to itself. They can disagree: fewer instructions pay when threads share a core (experiment 22), a stall avoided pays when they do not (experiment 24). The track page says each run gets "8 pinned vCPUs (hyperthreads)", so the scorer is the first case, 4 cores with two threads each: **the 4-thread number is the one to believe.**
- **bench.sh 20M** is `scripts/bench.sh 20M` on the development Mac (ARM, 8 threads, the organizers' generator, seed 120): best / median wall ms of 5. The Mac is under memory pressure and its numbers wander by 10% or more; they are here because the rules ask for one, not because they decide anything.
- **Verified** means: 36 fixtures, `make check-dev`, `scripts/diff_vs_ref.sh` on seeds 2 to 5, on the native build and on an x86-64 build (`scripts/regress.sh`), then CI on real amd64.

With it: 40 rounds on each build, 0 failures. **The pipeline does not define which of two different copies with one `ingest_ts` wins, and its answer there depends on thread timing.** That is within the rules, and 8 scored submissions out of 8 are what one would expect if the hidden data keeps the promise; if a submission ever fails with one event more or less on a user's day, look here first.

## The probe (branch `probe`)

Not for scoring. It runs the pipeline and then makes `user_daily` empty so that the dev check fails: a failed dev check is the only case in which the track page shows a submission its log. A rejected submission does not change the best score; it does use one of the two submissions an hour. Merge `main` into it before use; the commit to hand over is in the session's report, not here, because it changes with every merge.

What it logs, and what each answer decides (first in the log is first here, in case a long log is cut):
- **the parse phase of the dev set cut short at each stage, 8 threads** (read, + delimiter scan, + line walk, + fields into values, + link lookups, + event records, everything): where parse time goes on the scorer's CPU, to set against the same list from GitHub's machines (the `anatomy` measurement above);
- **the whole parse phase on 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 16 threads**, and `read()` + scan alone: whether the 8 CPUs are 8 cores or 4 cores' hyperthreads, so whether the 4-thread or the one-thread column of this file is the one to believe, and whether memory bandwidth caps the scan;
- the CPU model and its AVX-512 subsets, cgroup CPU and memory limits, `cpu.stat` (was the run throttled), huge-page settings, every mount, the environment; and, once `main` is merged into it, **which scan loop the scorer runs** (the log's `scan narrow` or `scan avx512`, experiment 29);
- when the sandbox's files and the process were created: how long a sandbox takes to reach `main` (experiment 16);
- the same spin loop on 1 to 16 threads: real CPUs, without memory in the picture;
- **256 MB touched and unmapped under each huge-page advice, on 1 and 8 threads**: what 4 KB pages cost here going in and at exit. The pipeline touches 1.2 GB at 20M events and 3.4 GB at 60M;
- `/output`, `/scratch` and `/tmp` write and read speed: whether a smaller output matters;
- `read()` against `mmap()` on the largest event file: whether experiment 10 should be retried;
- 256-bit against 512-bit scans, alone and alternating with scalar work: whether a 512-bit stage one pays.

## To try, most promising first

`main` (`eb851d8`) is the last submitted commit (`8527c64`) plus experiments 30, 31, 32 and 33: 4.6 and 4.7% ahead of it at 20M on Zen 4, the four measured together. An unattended cycle submits again when `main` is 5% ahead in both repeats (and see "The 5% rule and a gain that depends on the CPU" for what that meant in cycle 4): **one more kept change of about half a percent would do it.** The percentages below are shares of samples in cycle 7's two profiles, 4 threads at 20M: the first on Zen 5 before 32 (`3555e71`), the second on Zen 3 after it (`c0022d5`); no profile was taken after 33. The gains are estimates, not measurements.

1. **Read the scorer's machine**: the probe's log, if the user spends a submission on it (it has not been submitted; an unattended cycle never submits it, because it fails the dev check on purpose). The track page already answers one of its questions: the 8 CPUs are hyperthreads. Still open, and worth more now that half of each gain goes missing on the scorer: how long a sandbox takes to reach `main`, what 4 KB pages cost there going in and at exit, **whether the scorer gives huge pages at all** (its log has the settings; experiments 12, 13 and 32 are worth nothing where it does not), how fast `/output` is, and whether the scorer has AVX-512 VBMI2, so whether experiment 29 is running there at all.
2. **More of parse on AVX-512, behind the same run-time check** (`scan::chosen`), if the probe or a score says the scorer has it; not before, because only CI can test it and only one runner in three has it. Candidates, none measured: the field split with mask registers (no `movemask`, no insert chain: a gather of the 4 bytes around each delimiter); both IDs of a line packed at once with `vpermb`/`vpmultishiftqb` (VBMI) instead of two rounds of nibble tables and `pext`. Parse is 64% of the run and stage one is now a small part of it.
3. **`mmap` again, if the scorer is bare metal.** It lost on virtual machines because of page-table walks (experiment 10); on bare metal those are cheap, and the copy it saves is 3 to 4% of the samples.
4. **The join's fetches** (new in cycle 8; 1.7 to 2.1% of the run at 20M, 1.9% at 60M, by the log's `join`). For each edge the join asks for the two IDs' home lines 8 edges ahead, which works; then `find` follows each ID's parent pointer, and the parent's, to lines nobody asked for, and the CAS that hangs one root under the other waits on them. A ring like the parser's would overlap those too: ask for the home lines at edge i + 16, claim the two slots and ask for their parents' lines at i + 8, join at i. The order of the joins does not change the answer (a tree's root is its smallest ID in any order, which is what the unit tests of 33 hold it to on 1 to 8 threads), so a ring is safe. Naming got 60% faster from the same medicine; half of the join would be about 1% of the run. Not measured. Cheap to try beside it, with a knob: the table half as large (item 8), which also halves the 64 MB (256 MB at 60M) of fresh pages the join touches first.
5. **`cells.credited`**, 1.9% and 1.15% on the two lines that add a session to it (`person.rs:478` and `:500`): 672 KB a thread (24 bytes for each campaign and day), read at random, once a session. A design from cycle 8, on paper: the campaign a session is credited to always assists it too (rules.md, clicks, step 7), so its `assisted` counter is written a moment before; with the session count beside that counter, 8 bytes a cell and 224 KB a thread for both, crediting a session fetches no line of its own. Purchases and revenue, which most sessions do not have, in a second array written through a select (a scratch cell for a session without a purchase). `write_campaign_daily` in `main.rs` reads the cells. Up to 1% by estimate.
6. **The sort of each person's clicks**, 2.0% and 1.4% (two sort functions, the rest in small-sort lines). The comparison is two-level (time, then `click_id` through `key::compare`); ties in time are rare. Sort by the first word alone, which is a plain `u64`, then walk once and put the few runs of equal time in `click_id` order. Not the counting pass of 26.
7. **Timestamps with an offset** (one line in ten): `ts::parse_general` is 1.7% and 1.6%, the branch that leads to it (`ts.rs:331`) 0.3% and 1.6%, the call (`ts.rs:334`) 0.7% and 0.6%. The date and the time of day have already been worked out when that branch is taken; a fallback for the offset form that takes them as arguments does not do it again. Making the offset form branch-free for every line was costed on paper and is not worth trying: about 30 more instructions on every line to save about 100 on one line in ten.
8. **What the lines around the link lookup wait for.** `links.rs:43` (after the table's prefetch) 2.15% and 1.23%, `links.rs:57` and `:58` (the table's line read) 2.3% and 1.75%, the line after the prefetch of a partition's state 1.43% and 1.38%, `_mm_prefetch` 1.41% and 1.35%: 5 to 7% of the samples on a few dozen instructions a line, before and after 32 ("What 32 says"). The link table is 64 MB at 20M (2^22 slots for 890,000 link lines and 1.19 million IDs on the Mac's set), under a third full, read once a line. Cheap things to measure, each a knob and one sweep, at 60M too: the table half as large (never tried; 7 tried it twice as large and building it cost more than it saved); `LOOK` and `RING` (12 and 16 lines since 24) as settings; the state's prefetch left out, since the states are 16 KB a stream and may be in cache anyway.
9. **Touch less memory.** The kernel is 12.7 to 14.5% of the CPU time on the Intel runners and 7 to 10% on the AMD ones ("What 30 says", "What 31 says", "What 32 says"), and what it does is copy file data and zero new pages: about 1.1 GB of them at 20M since 31, which was the first piece of this item (`user_daily` without its copies) and gave 1.4 to 2.3% on Intel. Two pieces are left, each its own experiment, each to be checked at 60M because it touches memory layout:
   (b) The unused ends of chunks. On the Mac at 20M the pools hand out slabs for 58.5 million records and 47.3 million are written. About 3 million of the difference is the unused ends of slabs, which are never touched; the rest is the unused ends of chunks, and under huge pages those are zeroed with the page they lie in: roughly 150 MB at 20M (worked out from the log's counts, not measured). `main.rs` rounds the chunk size up to a power of two; a quarter of it would leave a quarter of that waste, for four times the chunks to walk. No knob for it yet: add one and use the workflow's sweep.
   (c) The copy records are dead once dedup is done: 341 MB at 20M. What the people phase allocates afterwards could be carved from those slabs instead of from new pages. Since 31 that is only its rows, about 80 MB at 20M (worked out from the row count, not measured), so this is worth less than half of what it was.
10. **Dedup**, 45 ms of 800 at 20M (Zen 4): two scans of the copy records with a hash each, and the table of 30. What would be larger is **dedup without a record for every copy**: 97% of copy records only prove that an event has no second copy. They cost a push a line (about 10% of parse) and 341 MB of new pages. Still no design, and gone over again in cycles 5 and 7 on paper, nothing built: (i) a shared bitmap, tested and set atomically for each line, fetches a 64-byte line at random for each line where the push writes 16 bytes in order, and still needs a second pass to tell the first copy of each pair; (ii) copy records of a 4-byte fingerprint need the event's key and `ingest_ts` in the event record for the suspects, 36 bytes a line instead of today's 32, and a third phase in which suspects from different person partitions meet; (iii) one 32-byte record filed by event, events scattered to their people after dedup, is the same two pushes a line and 320 MB more; (iv) a 12-byte copy record does not fit: 54 bits of key (the partition gives the other ten, if the hash is one that can be undone), 32 of `ingest_ts` and 27 of index are 113. The two-stream layout looks like the right one; the copy record itself needs all 16 of its bytes.
11. **Instruction cuts left in parse**, 1 to 2% each: both IDs of a line packed in one 256-bit operation (`key.rs` is 3.5% of the instructions callgrind counts; about 18 instructions a line fewer by a count on paper); the hash worked out once (`sys::mix` is 2 to 3% of the samples: a line's person and event are hashed again in dedup and in the people phase, which a key stored already hashed, by a hash that can be undone, would not need); the `ts` flags; field bounds read from memory instead of extracted from a vector register.
12. **The people phase's grouping**: the scatter of events to their people (`person.rs:276` and `:277`) 1.1% and 1.8%, the scatter into bins 0.4% and 1.0%, the hash probes that number the people about 1%. A partition's working set there is about 850 KB (records, two staging arrays, the result; worked out from the record counts, not measured), more than a Zen 3 core's 512 KB second-level cache; partition counts were swept and made no difference (fewer partitions make parse faster and people slower by as much). Its small arrays (the people's numbers, cursors and starts, under 32 KB each) are the system allocator's, on 4 KB pages, about 20 of them a thread: by the reading of 32 that is too few to matter, and it is not measured. No design; 26 showed that an extra pass costs more than a sort.
13. **Smaller output.** `user_daily` is 78 MB of PLAIN Parquet at 20M events. Dictionary and run-length encodings would cut it to about a half without touching `revenue_cents` (a person's rows follow one another, so `user_id` is one run a person; `day` has 14 values; `sessions` and `events` mostly fit a byte): worked out on paper, nothing built. The kernel's side of the write was 0.26% of samples on Sapphire Rapids and 0.2 to 0.5% on the AMD runners: worth little inside the process. Outside it, GitHub's Docker took about 35 ms longer to end a container that had written 222 MB to disk than one that wrote to memory (experiment 17, 60M), and 10 to 23 ms longer for 78 MB in one of the two 20M measurements, nothing in the other; whether the scorer's sandbox does is one more thing only the probe or a score can say, and every encoding other than PLAIN is one more thing the checker's reader has to get right.
14. **For the AVX2 scan loop only** (the one that runs where there is no AVX-512): the delimiter bitmap from one table lookup and one compare for every 32 bytes (`pshufb` by the low nibble, then "equal to the byte itself") instead of two compares and an OR: two instructions a block fewer. The wide loop already does it (the table is `DELIMITER_BY_LOW_NIBBLE` in `src/scan.rs`).
