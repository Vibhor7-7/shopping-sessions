# BENCH.md: every experiment, kept or not

## How to read this

- **Scorer** is the only number that counts: the track page's time for a commit, on the hidden ~20M-event practice set.
- **x86 A/B** is what decides whether a change is kept: the `bench` workflow builds two commits with the Dockerfile and runs them interleaved on one GitHub x86-64 machine (2 cores / 4 threads, stand-in 20M-event dataset from `src/bin/synth.rs`). Times are the in-process total in ms, minimum of 5 runs, base → head. Noise between repeats is about 1%. The CPU differs from run to run, so only compare within a row.
- The workflow's 4-thread number has two threads to a core; its **one-thread** number has a core to itself. They can disagree: fewer instructions pay when threads share a core (experiment 22), a stall avoided pays when they do not (experiment 24). The track page says each run gets "8 pinned vCPUs (hyperthreads)", so the scorer is the first case, 4 cores with two threads each: **the 4-thread number is the one to believe.**
- **bench.sh 20M** is `scripts/bench.sh 20M` on the development Mac (ARM, 8 threads, the organizers' generator, seed 120): best / median wall ms of 5. The Mac is under memory pressure and its numbers wander by 10% or more; they are here because the rules ask for one, not because they decide anything.
- **Verified** means: 36 fixtures, `make check-dev`, `scripts/diff_vs_ref.sh` on seeds 2 to 5, on the native build and on an x86-64 build (`scripts/regress.sh`), then CI on real amd64.

## The probe (branch `probe`)

Not for scoring. It runs the pipeline and then makes `user_daily` empty so that the dev check fails: a failed dev check is the only case in which the track page shows a submission its log. A rejected submission does not change the best score; it does use one of the two submissions an hour. Merge `main` into it before use; the commit to hand over is in the session's report, not here, because it changes with every merge.

What it logs, and what each answer decides (first in the log is first here, in case a long log is cut):
- **the parse phase of the dev set cut short at each stage, 8 threads** (read, + delimiter scan, + line walk, + fields into values, + link lookups, + event records, everything): where parse time goes on the scorer's CPU, to set against the same list from GitHub's machines (the `anatomy` measurement above);
- **the whole parse phase on 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 16 threads**, and `read()` + scan alone: whether the 8 CPUs are 8 cores or 4 cores' hyperthreads, so whether the 4-thread or the one-thread column of this file is the one to believe, and whether memory bandwidth caps the scan;
- the CPU model and its AVX-512 subsets, cgroup CPU and memory limits, `cpu.stat` (was the run throttled), huge-page settings, every mount, the environment; and, once `main` is merged into it, **which scan loop the scorer runs** (the log's `scan narrow` or `scan avx512`, experiment 29);
- when the sandbox's files and the process were created: how long a sandbox takes to reach `main` (experiment 16);
- the same spin loop on 1 to 16 threads: real CPUs, without memory in the picture;
- **256 MB touched and unmapped under each huge-page advice, on 1 and 8 threads**: what 4 KB pages cost here going in and at exit. The pipeline touches 1.2 GB at 20M events and 3.4 GB at 60M;
- `/output`, `/scratch` and `/tmp` write and read speed: whether a smaller output matters;
- `read()` against `mmap()` on the largest event file: whether experiment 10 should be retried;
- 256-bit against 512-bit scans, alone and alternating with scalar work: whether a 512-bit stage one pays.

3. **`mmap` again, if the scorer is bare metal.** It lost on virtual machines because of page-table walks (experiment 10); on bare metal those are cheap, and the copy it saves is 3 to 5% of the samples.
4. **Touch less memory.** The kernel is 14.5% of the CPU time on the Intel runner and 7% on the AMD ones ("What 30 says"), and what it does is copy file data and zero new pages: 1.2 GB of them at 20M. Three pieces, each its own experiment, each to be checked at 60M because it touches memory layout:
   (a) `user_daily` without its copies. The rows are kept as keys (18 MB), decoded into an ID buffer (29 MB) a byte at a time, and all columns are then copied into one 78 MB buffer for a single `write`. `key::write_id` alone is **1.0%** of samples. Decode 8 characters at once (`pdep` is `squeeze` backwards, then one table shuffle; write 16 bytes, advance by the length), do it as each row is made, and write the file from its columns with `writev`. About 100 MB fewer new pages and the 1%.
   (b) The unused ends of chunks. On the Mac at 20M the pools hand out slabs for 58.98 million records and 47.30 million are written. About 3 million of the difference is the unused ends of slabs, which are never touched; the rest is the unused ends of chunks, and under huge pages those are zeroed with the page they lie in: roughly 150 MB at 20M (worked out from the log's counts, not measured). `main.rs` rounds the chunk size up to a power of two; a quarter of it would leave a quarter of that waste, for four times the chunks to walk. No knob for it yet: add one and use the workflow's sweep.
   (c) The copy records are dead once dedup is done: 341 MB at 20M. What the people phase allocates afterwards (its rows, about 180 MB with the output buffers of (a)) could be carved from those slabs instead of from new pages.
5. **The sort of each person's clicks**, about 1.9% (0.88% + 0.55% in two sort functions, the rest in small-sort lines). The comparison is two-level (time, then `click_id` through `key::compare`); ties in time are rare. Sort by the first word alone, which is a plain `u64`, then walk once and put the few runs of equal time in `click_id` order. Not the counting pass of 26.
6. **`cells.credited`**, 1.2% on the two lines that add a session to it (`person.rs:475` and `:497`): 672 KB a thread, read at random, once a session. 16-byte cells (two `u32` counts and the revenue), or a short log of (cell, purchased, revenue) applied in batches behind a prefetch.
7. **Timestamps with an offset** (one line in ten): `ts::parse_general` is 1.6% and the branch that leads to it, `ts.rs:331`, 1.4%. The date and the time of day have already been worked out when that branch is taken; a fallback for the offset form that takes them as arguments does not do it again. Making the offset form branch-free for every line was costed on paper and is not worth trying: about 30 more instructions on every line to save about 100 on one line in ten.
8. **Links: name in one pass.** Naming is two passes over the whole 64 MB table (16 to 18 ms of 1070 at 20M on 4 threads, as long as the join). If a parent pointer and a name can be told apart in the word they share (a tag in the low four bits that no key has), one pass can replace each pointer by the name directly, whatever the other threads have already replaced.
9. **Dedup**, now 46 ms of 836 at 20M (Granite Rapids): two scans of the copy records with a hash each, and the table of 30. What would be larger is **dedup without a record for every copy**: 97% of copy records only prove that an event has no second copy. They cost a push a line (about 10% of parse) and 341 MB of new pages. Still no design, and gone over again in cycle 5 on paper, nothing built: (i) a shared bitmap, tested and set atomically for each line, fetches a 64-byte line at random for each line where the push writes 16 bytes in order, and still needs a second pass to tell the first copy of each pair; (ii) copy records of a 4-byte fingerprint need the event's key and `ingest_ts` in the event record for the suspects, 36 bytes a line instead of today's 32, and a third phase in which suspects from different person partitions meet; (iii) one 32-byte record filed by event, events scattered to their people after dedup, is the same two pushes a line and 320 MB more. The two-stream layout looks like the right one; the copy record itself needs all 16 of its bytes.
10. **Instruction cuts left in parse**, 1 to 2% each: both IDs of a line packed in one 256-bit operation (`key.rs` is 4.5% of samples); the `ts` flags; field bounds read from memory instead of extracted from a vector register.
11. **The people phase's grouping**: the scatter of events to their people is 1.8% (`person.rs:273` and `:274`), the scatter into bins 1.2%, the hash probes that number the people 1.7%. A partition's working set there is about 850 KB (records, two staging arrays, the result; worked out from the record counts, not measured), more than a Zen 3 core's 512 KB second-level cache; partition counts were swept and made no difference (fewer partitions make parse faster and people slower by as much). No design; 26 showed that an extra pass costs more than a sort.
12. **Smaller output.** `user_daily` is 78 MB of PLAIN Parquet at 20M events. Dictionary and run-length encodings would cut it to about a fifth. The kernel's side of the write was 0.26% of samples on Sapphire Rapids and under 0.2% on Zen 3: worth little unless the probe shows the scorer's `/output` is slow.
13. **For the AVX2 scan loop only** (the one that runs where there is no AVX-512): the delimiter bitmap from one table lookup and one compare for every 32 bytes (`pshufb` by the low nibble, then "equal to the byte itself") instead of two compares and an OR: two instructions a block fewer.
